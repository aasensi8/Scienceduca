Atomic models from Democritus to today

Open a milestone to see what was proposed or discovered and what evidence explains it.

5th century BCE · Democritus

The idea of the atom

Democritus proposed that dividing matter would eventually lead to indivisible particles called atoms. This was a philosophical idea. It was not based on experimental measurements.

Early 19th century · John Dalton

Atomic theory of matter

Dalton proposed that each element has its own type of atom and that atoms combine in simple whole-number ratios. This explained the mass ratios observed in reactions and compounds.

1897 · J. J. Thomson

Discovery of the electron

Thomson noticed that electric and magnetic fields bent cathode rays as expected for a beam of negatively charged particles. These were electrons, particles smaller than atoms.

1904 · J. J. Thomson

Model with embedded electrons

To explain how an atom with electrons could be neutral, Thomson proposed that positive charge was spread throughout the atom, with electrons embedded in it. The positive and negative charges balanced.

1909–1911 · Geiger, Marsden and Rutherford

Gold foil and the nucleus

How was the experiment set up?

Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden, working with Ernest Rutherford, aimed a beam of positively charged alpha particles at very thin gold foil. A fluorescent screen gave a small flash where a particle hit it.

What did they observe?

Most particles passed through with little or no change in direction. A small fraction changed direction sharply and some came back towards the source.

What did Rutherford conclude?

Thomson’s model, with positive charge spread out, predicted mainly small changes in direction. In 1911 Rutherford proposed that the positive charge and almost all the mass were concentrated in a tiny nucleus. Particles passing close to it were strongly repelled. Most passed far from it and went almost straight through.

1913 · Niels Bohr

Energy levels

Bohr kept Rutherford’s nucleus and proposed that electrons could only occupy certain orbits with fixed energies. When an electron moves to a lower level, the atom emits light with energy equal to the difference between the levels. This explained the lines in the hydrogen spectrum.

In the modern quantum model, shells are still linked to energy, and electron positions are described using probabilities.

1919–1920 · Ernest Rutherford

Identification of the proton

When Rutherford bombarded nitrogen with alpha particles, hydrogen nuclei were released. He published the results in 1919. In 1920 he proposed the name proton for the nucleus of ordinary hydrogen. The number of protons identifies the element.

1926 · Erwin Schrödinger

Quantum model

Schrödinger wrote an equation that describes electrons using a wave function. Max Born’s interpretation connects this function to the probability of finding an electron in a particular region. These distributions are linked to orbitals.

1932 · James Chadwick

Discovery of the neutron

When alpha particles hit beryllium, they produced radiation that could knock protons out of paraffin. Chadwick analysed the energy of these protons and concluded that the radiation contained neutral particles with a mass similar to a proton. Different neutron counts give different isotopes of the same element.

1964 to the late 1960s · Gell-Mann, Zweig and scattering experiments

Quarks

In 1964 Murray Gell-Mann and George Zweig independently proposed that protons and neutrons contain quarks. Later, electron scattering experiments at SLAC found evidence of smaller components inside the proton.

The proton has two up quarks and one downquark. The neutron has one up quark and two downquarks. The electron is an elementary particle.

Today · Quantum model and Standard Model

How do we describe the atom today?

We describe an atom as a nucleus with protons and usually neutrons, surrounded by electrons in quantum states. Orbitals show the probability distribution for each electron.

At a smaller scale, the Standard Model describes electrons and quarks as elementary particles and gluons as carriers of the strong interaction. Each model answers questions at a different scale.

Matter and electricity

If matter contains charged particles, why are many objects neutral?

When does an object have a net charge?

In a neutral object, positive and negative charges balance. If it gains electrons, it becomes negatively charged. If it loses electrons, it becomes positively charged.

A neutral atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons. Neutrons add mass and have zero charge.

How the particles were discovered

  • e⁻
    1897 · J. J. Thomson. Experiments with cathode rays provided evidence for the electron.
  • p⁺
    1919 · Ernest Rutherford. He published results showing that hydrogen nuclei were produced when nitrogen was bombarded with alpha particles. A normal hydrogen nucleus is a proton.
  • n⁰
    1932 · James Chadwick. He identified the neutron, a particle with no charge and a mass similar to that of a proton.
Explore quarks

Protons and neutrons contain quarks. Their valence quarks explain their net charge. The two types involved are up (u), with charge +2/3, and down (d), with charge −1/3.

Gluons carry the strong interaction that holds quarks together. The electron is an elementary particle in the lepton family. The circles in the diagram help you count the valence quarks.

Calculate the mass and charge of an atom

What is relative charge? It is charge measured in units of the elementary charge e (about 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C). A proton counts as +1, an electron as −1 and a neutron as 0. Subtract the number of electrons from the number of protons. For example, three protons and two electrons give +1, so the charge is +1e.

An atom has four protons, four neutrons and four electrons.

  1. Estimate its mass in kg by adding the particle masses. What mass do you get if you remove all four electrons?
  2. What is its approximate mass in u? What was its relative charge at the start?
  3. What charge would it have if it gained one electron? What if the neutral atom lost two electrons instead?

Equipment. A balance, a small container, a dropper and a water sample chosen by your team, such as distilled, tap or bottled water. Atomic structure

The nucleus contains positively charged protons and usually neutrons, which have no charge. Electrons are negatively charged and are found in the region around the nucleus.

  • Atomic number (Z) = number of protons. It identifies the element.
  • Mass number (A) = protons + neutrons.
  • Charge in elementary units = protons − electrons.

Monatomic ions

A monatomic ion is charged because the number of electrons differs from the number of protons.

Worked example: sodium-23

When Z = 11 and A = 23, there are 11 protons and 23 − 11 = 12 neutrons. A neutral atom has 11 electrons. The Na⁺ ion has 10.

Carbon isotopes

Isotopes of the same element have the same number of protons (Z) and different numbers of neutrons. Their mass numbers (A) are therefore different. All three carbon isotopes below have six protons. Each diagram shows a neutral atom with six electrons.

Atomic masses of the elements

The mass number (A) counts protons and neutrons. The average atomic mass is a weighted average of the isotope masses, measured in atomic mass units (u).

Worked example: carbon

In a simplified sample, about 99% of the atoms are carbon-12 and 1% are carbon-13. We omit the traces of carbon-14 and use rounded isotope masses of 12 u and 13 u.

Average atomic mass ≈ 99×12 u+1×13 u100 = 12.01 u.

The result is much closer to 12 u because carbon-12 is much more abundant.

Electron configuration

Electron configuration shows how electrons are arranged. In the Bohr model we draw shells as circles around the nucleus. Each shell corresponds to an energy level. We will use this diagram for the first 20 elements.

How do we arrange the electrons?

  1. Find the atomic number Z. In a neutral atom, the number of electrons equals Z.
  2. Place up to two electrons in the first shell and up to eight in the second.
  3. For the first 20 elements, place up to eight electrons in the third shell and any remaining electrons in the fourth.

Why does the fourth shell start filling before the third is full?

Electrons occupy the lowest-energy states first. In potassium and calcium, the 4s subshell fills before the 3d subshell. So the third shell has eight electrons in these examples, even though it can hold up to 18. The fourth shell can hold up to 32.

The circles help us count electrons by shell. The quantum model uses orbitals and probabilities to describe electrons.

Check your prediction about strontium

The common ion is Sr²⁺. Strontium is a group 2 alkaline earth metal, like magnesium and calcium. It commonly has a charge of +2 in compounds.

Metals and non-metals

Metals generally conduct heat and electricity well, and many form cations. Non-metals have a range of properties. For example, halogens often form ions with charge −1. Graphite is an exception to the usual pattern for conductivity. It is a form of carbon that conducts electricity.

Abundance of the elements

Abundance depends on the place or sample. Hydrogen and helium are the most abundant elements in the universe. Closer to home, composition depends on the material. Pure water contains hydrogen and oxygen. Table salt mainly contains sodium and chloride ions.

Key vocabulary

Matter
Anything that has mass and occupies space.
Atom
The smallest unit of an element that can take part in a chemical change.
Element
A substance defined by the number of protons in its atoms.
Nucleus
The small, dense centre of an atom, containing protons and usually neutrons.
Proton
A positively charged particle in the nucleus.
Neutron
A particle in the nucleus with no electrical charge.
Electron
A negatively charged elementary particle found around the nucleus.
Atomic number (Z)
The number of protons. It identifies the element.
Mass number (A)
The total number of protons and neutrons in a nucleus.
Isotope
One of the forms of an element with the same proton count and a different neutron count.
Ion
An atom or group of atoms that has gained or lost electrons and has a net charge.
Cation and anion
A cation is a positive ion. An anion is a negative ion.
Relative charge
Charge measured in units of the elementary charge. A proton has relative charge +1 and an electron −1.
Atomic mass unit (u)
One twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom.
Molecule
A group of atoms joined by chemical bonds, such as H₂O.
Electron shell
A group of electron states with the same principal energy-level number.
Electron configuration
The arrangement of electrons in the available shells and subshells.
Orbital
A description of the probability of finding an electron in different regions.
Valence electron
For the elements used here, an electron in the outermost occupied shell that can affect bonding.
Group
A column of the periodic table.
Period
A row of the periodic table.
Quark
An elementary particle that helps make up protons and neutrons.
Uncertainty
The estimated range of doubt associated with a measurement.
